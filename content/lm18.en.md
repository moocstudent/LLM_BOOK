## The mechanisms

**Full fine-tuning**: update every weight. Highest ceiling, especially with large data and genuinely new capabilities. The price is memory, one full set of weights per task, and the highest forgetting risk.

**LoRA / QLoRA**: train two low-rank matrices, mergeable into the original weights at inference. Today's default.

**Prefix tuning**: leave the weights alone and train only the key/value representations of a short run of "virtual tokens" prepended at every layer's attention input. Fewest parameters (often only a few hundred thousand), but it **consumes part of the context window** and clearly trails LoRA on hard tasks.

**Adapter layers**: insert a small bottleneck network (down-project, activate, up-project) between Transformer layers. Solid quality, and historically earlier than LoRA.

## Why LoRA won

The most informative column in the comparison is **inference cost**:

- Adapter layers change the computation graph, adding a forward pass per layer. That overhead **cannot be removed**.
- Prefix tuning processes those virtual tokens on every forward pass and occupies context. Equally unremovable.
- LoRA is literally a low-rank term added to the weights, so **it can be merged into W at deployment** and become an entirely ordinary model.

In `W' = W + (α/r)·B·A`, the right-hand side can be collapsed into a single matrix before deployment. A merged model is indistinguishable from any ordinary model: zero added latency, runs in any framework, needs no special support.

**That property decided the competition.** In research, the quality differences among these methods are a few points; in deployment, "mergeable or not" is the difference between possible and impossible.

## Merge or load dynamically

The interesting part is that LoRA's other half of value comes precisely from **not** merging.

Unmerged, the base weights can be shared across many adapters. One machine loads a single 14 GB 7B base and hosts thirty 20 MB adapters, serving thirty customers' dedicated models concurrently. Switching customers swaps a matrix, in milliseconds.

The experiment above contrasts the two deployment shapes:

| | Merged into weights | Dynamically loaded |
|---|---|---|
| Inference latency | zero added | +2%–5% |
| Memory for 30 customers | 30 × 14 GB | 14 GB + 30 × 20 MB |
| Switching customers | reload the whole model | milliseconds |
| Framework requirement | any | multi-adapter support |
| Suits | one model for everyone; weights handed to the customer | multi-tenant SaaS |

**These are not better and worse; they are two different business shapes.** If you build multi-customer SaaS, dynamic loading turns "a dedicated model per customer" from a cost joke into standard practice — perhaps LoRA's largest effect on what products can exist.

## On stacking adapters

A common hope: I have a "legal tone" LoRA and a "answer in Chinese" LoRA — can I stack them?

**Usually not.** Each was trained against the base model and there is no reason their update directions are orthogonal. Summed directly, the two ΔW interfere, often producing a model worse at both.

Research exists on composing adapters (orthogonality constraints, routed mixtures), but in engineering practice, **to have both behaviours, retrain one adapter on a mixture of both datasets.** That also matches the previous module's data principle: consistency comes from a single standard, and two independently trained adapters are inherently two standards.

## Version management

One last practically important point that is rarely discussed: adapters need version management.

An adapter's reproducibility depends on four things: **the exact base-model version, the exact training-data snapshot, the hyperparameter configuration, and the tokenizer.** The first causes the most trouble — the base model ships a minor version bump and your adapter may no longer match, with the symptom being inexplicably worse output rather than an error.

Recommendation: store hashes of all four alongside the adapter and verify the base version at load time. Dull advice, and it will save you hours of confusion one day.
