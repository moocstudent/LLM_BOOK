## "It feels better now" is the most expensive sentence

The fine-tune finishes, you look at ten samples, the output reads nicer, and success is declared — the most widespread self-deception in this field.

Three reasons:

1. **Ten samples carry almost no information.** We will compute that with confidence intervals below.
2. **You are cherry-picking.** People validating their own work unconsciously look at the examples they wanted to improve.
3. **The total hides regressions.** Pass rate from 68% to 72% may be 8 items improved and 4 broken. And those 4 are exactly what the customer will remember.

## The golden eval set

The first thing you need is a golden set of 50–200 items. Four requirements:

**One: items come from real user questions.** Not questions you imagined. If the product has not launched, use the closest real source: actual scenarios raised in customer interviews, historical tickets, questions users ask a competitor.

**Two: each item carries an input, the expected key points, and a grading method.** Note "key points", not "the expected answer" — on free-text tasks a unique answer does not exist, so list the 2–5 points that must appear.

**Three: cover edge cases.** Especially these three, which are almost always omitted: inputs where the answer is absent, items whose answer is "uncertain", and inputs that are internally contradictory. Those three are the main source of production incidents.

**Four: split it into a dev set and a held-out set.** The dev set you may use repeatedly for tuning; the held-out set is spent once at final acceptance. The reason appears under "overfitting your own eval" below.

## Four grading methods

| Method | Suits | Cost | Trap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exact match | classification, extraction, numbers | very low | unusable on free text |
| Key-point coverage | free text | low | points must be written in advance, never after |
| Model judge | long text, subjective quality | moderate | favours length and its own phrasing |
| Human | the hardest 10% | high | slow, and annotators must be aligned |

**Recommended default: key-point coverage.** Best value on free-text tasks, with a side benefit — writing the points forces you to decide what "good" means, which is often more valuable than the evaluation result itself.

**On model judges:** scalable, but they must be calibrated first. Have humans label 50 items and measure agreement between the judge and the humans. Below 80%, do not use it for acceptance — only for coarse screening.

## Confidence intervals: what those 4 points prove

This is the chapter's most practical section.

The 95% confidence half-width for a difference of two proportions is roughly:

```
half-width ≈ 1.96 · √( (p₁(1-p₁) + p₂(1-p₂)) / n )
```

With n=50 and a 68% baseline, the half-width is about **18%**.

Which means **on 50 items, any change smaller than 18 points is inside the noise.** That "+4%" you observed is statistically indistinguishable from zero — indeed from −4%.

To reliably detect a genuine 4-point difference you need roughly **1,000** items. That number is discouraging, and it has two practical uses:

- **Do not celebrate small gains.** And do not panic at small losses.
- **Chase large gains first.** A change worth 3 points cannot be validated at reasonable cost; a change worth 15 points shows up in 50 items.

## Two kinds of contamination

**One: training-data contamination.** Eval items entered the training data. Common when splitting one dataset into train and eval without semantic deduplication (two phrasings of the same ticket). Symptom: excellent eval scores and a visible gap after launch.

**Two: overfitting your own eval.** You tuned repeatedly against the same eval set, trying thirty configurations and keeping the highest score. Statistically that is thirty searches over the eval set, and **the maximum includes luck**. Hence the held-out set, spent once at the end.

This trap is subtler than the first because it needs no data leakage at all. It is the oldest mistake in machine learning wearing new clothes.

## Regression detection

The final discipline: **rerun the whole set on every change and report gains and regressions separately.**

The total pass rate is for management; the regressions are for you. Every regression deserves a look — not every one needs fixing, but you must know what you gave up.

In practice this needs only a simple table: item id, previous result, current result, change. Generate it automatically after each run and read it in five minutes. The habit pays for itself entirely the first time you discover the total rose five points while the most important class of customer question broke.

## Exercises

1. In the experiment, sweep n from 10 to 1,000 and watch the confidence half-width. Find the sample size needed to reliably detect a 5-point gain. Remember that number.
2. Write 20 eval items for your own task, each with an input, 2–5 expected key points, and a grading method. Include three edge cases specifically (no answer, uncertain, contradictory input). Writing those 20 will noticeably deepen your understanding of your own task.
3. Audit your current evaluation process: is there a held-out set? are regressions reported separately? If neither, every improvement number you currently report deserves a question mark.
