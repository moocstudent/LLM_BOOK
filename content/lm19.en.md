## The ceiling of supervised fine-tuning

SFT's training signal is "for this input, the reference answer is this". That signal has a structural limit: **it can tell the model what is correct but not which of two correct answers is better.**

And in reality "good" is largely comparative. Two answers to the same question are both factually right, but one is concise and one is verbose; one leads with the conclusion and one meanders for three paragraphs; one states its uncertainty explicitly and one glosses over it. You cannot teach those differences by writing a reference answer — a reference answer is singular, while what you want to convey is a preference ordering.

Preference optimization exists for that.

## The three-stage pipeline

**Stage one: SFT.** Train the base model on tens of thousands of human-written instruction/answer pairs so it learns to respond as an assistant. Its output serves twice: as the starting point for what follows, and as the **reference model** in the KL penalty.

**Stage two: the reward model.** Sample two answers to the same prompt, have a person mark which is better, and train a scoring model on those pairwise comparisons.

Why pairwise rather than absolute scores? Because **people are good at comparing and bad at scoring**. "This answer is a 7.5" barely aligns across annotators, while agreement on "A beats B" is far higher. The reward model's loss simply asks it to score the chosen answer higher:

```
L = -log σ( r(x, y⁺) - r(x, y⁻) )
```

**Stage three: policy optimization.** The model generates answers, the reward model scores them, and the policy is updated on those scores (usually with PPO), with a KL penalty leashing the policy near the SFT model:

```
objective = E[ r(x, y) ] - β · KL( π || π_ref )
```

## The middle stage is the fragile one

This is the chapter's most important sentence: **the reward model is an approximation fitted to tens of thousands of human comparisons, and policy optimization is literally a search for its maximum.**

Any approximation reveals the gap between itself and true intent under maximal optimisation. Concretely:

- The policy discovers the reward model prefers longer answers, so answers grow.
- It discovers a preference for polite, empathetic phrasing, so every reply opens with "That's a great question".
- It discovers a preference for bullets and headings, so even "what's today's date" arrives as three bullet points.

**The reward climbs while humans find the output steadily worse.** That is reward hacking, and the next chapter builds a whole lab for watching it.

The KL penalty exists precisely for this: it prices the act of leaving the reference model so the policy cannot drift arbitrarily far in pursuit of score. That β is a rein — **too loose and it drifts, too tight and you did not train.** Push it to both extremes in the experiment above and see for yourself.

## On reward-model accuracy

An easily misread metric. You will see reward models report figures like "78% accurate on held-out preference pairs", and that number has a ceiling: **on hard preference pairs, human annotators agree with each other only about 75%–80% of the time.**

So when your reward model reports accuracy above that, it is probably not better at judging quality but **fitting annotation bias** — having learned the shortcut that longer usually wins. The experiment above raises exactly that warning past 82%.

## The alignment tax

RLHF makes models safer, more polite and more uniform in format, and simultaneously more monotonous, more formulaic, and visibly worse at creative work. The diversity drop is measurable.

This is not a bug but the consequence of optimising a **single** reward signal: any maximised scalar squeezes out the dimensions it does not measure.

There is only one direction of mitigation: **encode preferences for the dimensions you do not want to lose into the preference data itself** — construct "equally correct but shorter" pairs to counter length inflation, construct "more original phrasing" pairs to preserve diversity. Which is why constructing preference data deserves more of your time than RLHF's algorithmic details.
