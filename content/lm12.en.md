## The only useful boundary: knowledge or behaviour

Someone gets this question wrong almost every day, and the boundary is clean:

- **Missing knowledge → retrieval.** Facts, policies, product specs, customer history. These change, need citations, need access filtering.
- **Missing behaviour → fine-tuning.** Output format, tone, domain judgement patterns, refusal boundaries. These cannot be stably described in a prompt, or are too expensive to describe.

The test: ask yourself, "if I pasted the answer verbatim into the prompt, would the model get it right?"

- **Yes** → you lack knowledge; use retrieval to find that passage and paste it in.
- **No** → you lack behaviour; fine-tuning may help.

That single criterion settles about 80% of routing decisions.

## Why injecting knowledge by fine-tuning is inefficient and dangerous

**Inefficient**: a fact needs to appear dozens of times in training data before the model reliably retains it. To make it remember five thousand product specs, the data volume required is astronomical. Retrieval simply puts them in an index — minutes of work.

**Dangerous**: three specific dangers.

First, **any change means retraining.** A price changes, a policy updates, and you face a fresh training task. Retrieval updates one record.

Second, **no citations.** Knowledge baked into weights has no source, and the model cannot say which document an answer came from. In any setting requiring audit or human verification, that is disqualifying on its own.

Third, **no access control.** Weights are global. You cannot prevent department A's users from seeing department B's data — that data is already in the weights. Retrieval naturally supports per-user filtering of results.

Note that the second and third are **structural**: not "works poorly" but "cannot be done". Which is why this boundary is a decision rather than a trade-off.

## Three things fine-tuning also cannot fix

Beyond knowledge, three problems fine-tuning does not help with:

**One: hallucination.** Its causes are decoding parameters, missing context, and the model not knowing what it does not know. Fine-tuning only makes it state the kind of thing in your data more confidently — **and if your training data never says "I am not sure", it never will.**

**Two: arithmetic and exact retrieval.** These are architectural limits (tokenization, no external memory), not undertraining. Give it a tool.

**Three: real-time information.** The weights are a snapshot of their training moment.

## Four decision dimensions

When the call genuinely needs weighing, look at these four:

| Dimension | Favours retrieval | Favours fine-tuning |
|---|---|---|
| Knowledge churn | daily/weekly | essentially static |
| Citations needed | yes | no |
| Behaviour idiosyncrasy | generic is fine | highly specific |
| Latency & cost pressure | relaxed | tight |

## The hybrid: the commonest right answer

In practice the best answer is usually both: **fine-tune the shape, retrieve the content.**

A typical compliance-review setup:

- Use **retrieval** to pull the relevant regulations and past cases, with citations.
- Use **fine-tuning** so the model organises its conclusion the way your reviewers do: verdict first, evidence second, uncertainty stated explicitly at edge cases.

Knowledge then updates daily while the output style stays stable and auditable. It also solves the cost problem in passing, because the format and conventions live in the weights and the prompt only has to carry the retrieved content.

**The order matters: do retrieval first, then consider fine-tuning.** Retrieval shows you results within two days, and those results tell you whether the remaining gap is a knowledge problem or a behaviour problem. Reverse the order and you will spend a week training a model only to discover the problem was on the retrieval side all along.
