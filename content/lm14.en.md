## Three quality dimensions

Fine-tuning outcomes are almost entirely determined by data, and data quality decomposes into three dimensions. Note that they combine **multiplicatively** — a fact that determines every priority judgement that follows.

**One: diversity — how many distinct input shapes are covered.**

200 examples spanning 20 input shapes beat 2,000 of the same shape. The model learns a mapping from input to output and works reliably only on input shapes you have shown it. Low diversity's direct consequence is **poor generalisation**: an excellent score on your task validation set, broken by a rephrasing.

**Two: consistency — whether similar inputs get the same style of output.**

The most overlooked and most lethal of the three. If two annotators differ in style — one writes expansive conclusions, one is terse — the model learns the average, and that average resembles neither.

**This deserves repeating: a consistency problem cannot be solved by adding data.** Ten thousand more inconsistent examples only make the model reliably produce the mush.

**Three: correctness — how many labels are simply wrong.**

The model learns them faithfully. Rule of thumb: **removing 100 mislabelled examples usually beats adding 1,000 correct ones** — because a mislabel does not merely fail to teach, it actively teaches the wrong thing.

## Where data comes from

Three sources, each with its own problem.

**Real logs.** Ideal in principle: the distribution is correct by construction and includes every edge case you would not have imagined. The problem is they carry inputs without outputs (or with the current system's inadequate outputs), so answers still have to be written or corrected by hand.

**Human writing.** Most controllable quality, highest cost. A practised annotator produces roughly 60–100 high-quality examples a day, so 1,000 examples is about 10–15 person-days. Worth remembering, because it determines how much time your project plan must reserve for data.

**Model synthesis.** Generate examples in bulk with a strong model. Fast, cheap, good at broadening coverage. Two traps:

- **Self-reinforcement.** The model learns another model's preferences, phrasing tics and blind spots — including the things it cannot do. The higher the synthetic share, the more visible this becomes.
- **Illusory diversity.** Synthetic data looks varied, but its variation spreads around the generating model's distribution and often misaligns with real user inputs.

Practical guidance: **use synthetic data to broaden coverage, but the standard must come from people.** Concretely: hand-write 200 seed examples that define the standard, expand from them with a model, then spot-check and correct by hand.

## Four kinds of example to purge

**One: inputs containing the answer.** Common when constructing data from logs and accidentally leaving a result field in the input. The model learns to copy, evaluates brilliantly, and collapses in production.

**Two: outputs violating your own rules.** If you require "output null when unsure" but the data contains dozens of unsure-yet-guessed examples, those dozens teach the model to ignore the rule.

**Three: overlap with the validation set.** Deduplicate — semantically, not just literally (two phrasings of the same example count as duplicates).

**Four: pathologically long or short examples.** They take a disproportionate share of the loss.

## That logarithmic curve

The curve in the experiment above is logarithmic, and that alone is worth remembering: **going from 100 to 1,000 examples helps far more than 1,000 to 10,000.**

So the correct order is **correct before plentiful**:

1. Write the annotation guideline (required fields, edge-case handling, tone standard).
2. Have two people label the same 50 items, compare, and revise the guideline.
3. Purge the mislabels.
4. Fill in the diversity gaps (find the input shapes you have not covered).
5. **Only then** consider adding volume.

"1,000 good beats 100,000 bad" is not a slogan; it follows from those multiplicative factors: consistency and noise multiply the whole score, and no amount of extra data multiplies it back.

## Exercises

1. In the experiment, drop consistency from 100% to 40% and note how much quality falls; then try to recover it by adding data. The point is to confirm first-hand that data cannot buy back consistency.
2. Take your existing (or planned) fine-tuning data and hand-review 30 random rows. Count: how many are wrong, how many differ in style from the rest, how many you cannot confidently judge yourself. That last number usually postpones a training run.
3. Write an annotation guideline, then have a colleague label 20 items by it while you label the same 20, and compare agreement. Below 80%, go back and revise the guideline — you just saved a failed fine-tune.
