## Solving for the reward

DPO's derivation can be explained without carrying the algebra.

RLHF's objective is "maximise reward without straying far from the reference model". That KL-constrained maximisation has a **closed-form optimum**:

```
π*(y|x) ∝ π_ref(y|x) · exp( r(x,y) / β )
```

That is, the optimal policy is entirely determined by the reference model and the reward function. And the relation inverts — the reward can be expressed by the policy:

```
r(x,y) = β · log( π*(y|x) / π_ref(y|x) ) + const
```

**Here is the key step:** since the reward can be written in terms of the policy, substitute it into the reward model's pairwise loss. After substitution the reward function disappears entirely, leaving only the policy and the reference:

```
L = -log σ( β · [ (log π(y⁺|x) - log π_ref(y⁺|x)) - (log π(y⁻|x) - log π_ref(y⁻|x)) ] )
```

That is the DPO loss. **No reward model, no sampling, no reinforcement learning.** Its shape is nearly that of a binary classification loss, and training is as simple as supervised fine-tuning.

## The engineering difference is decisive

| | RLHF (PPO) | DPO |
|---|---|---|
| Train a reward model | yes | no |
| Online sampling during training | yes | no |
| Models resident in memory | 4 (policy, reference, reward, value) | 2 (policy, reference) |
| Implementation complexity | high | low |
| Hyperparameter sensitivity | high (all of PPO's) | low (essentially just β) |
| Quality ceiling | slightly higher | close |
| Sensitivity to preference-data quality | moderate | **high** |

The first five rows explain DPO's rapid adoption: it turned a reinforcement-learning pipeline needing a dedicated team into a training script that finishes on one GPU.

The last row is its price, and deserves its own section.

## Why DPO leans harder on data quality

In RLHF the reward model is a buffer: it fits a smooth scoring function over tens of thousands of comparisons, averaging away the influence of any single mislabel.

DPO has no such layer. **Every preference pair enters the gradient directly.** One mislabelled pair is one wrong gradient update, and nothing smooths it.

Concrete consequences:

- **Pairs must be distinguishable.** Two equally good answers carry no signal, only noise.
- **Length bias is amplified directly.** If your annotators prefer longer answers — the most stubborn bias in human labelling — the model faithfully learns that longer is better and output inflates to twice the baseline.
- **You need enough pairs.** Below one or two thousand, DPO usually yields only noise-level gains.

The remedies are equally direct: **control for length while annotating** (pair answers of similar length), **explicitly construct "equally correct but shorter" pairs**, and watch mean output length as an evaluation metric in its own right.

## What β controls

β plays the same role in DPO as the KL coefficient in RLHF: it sets how far the policy may travel from its reference.

- **Small β (<0.05)**: a loose leash and large drift. The typical degeneration is learning the surface features of good answers — longer, more headings, more disclaimers — rather than better content.
- **Large β (>0.3)**: the policy is held firmly and the win-rate gain is limited. If you observe "DPO changed almost nothing", check β first and pair distinguishability second.
- **Common starting point: 0.1.**

The "implicit reward margin" in the experiment above is worth watching: it is the log-probability gap between chosen and rejected answers, i.e. the preference strength the model learned. Growing too fast is usually bad news — it often means the model found a surface feature that separates the two.

## After DPO

A family of variants followed DPO, worth knowing by name and by the problem each addresses:

- **IPO**: reshapes the loss to reduce DPO's overfitting under noisy data.
- **KTO**: needs no pairs at all, only single labels of "this one is good / this one is not" — much cheaper to collect.
- **ORPO**: merges SFT and preference optimization into one stage, dropping the reference model.
- **SimPO**: removes the reference model and uses a length-normalised reward.

Their shared direction is **further simplifying the pipeline and relaxing its demands on data**. But in your project, which variant you choose usually matters far less than the quality of the preference data itself — a point worth repeating.
