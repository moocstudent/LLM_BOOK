## Five ingredients, ordered by leverage

The genuinely useful components of a prompt, in descending order of empirical leverage:

**One: an explicit task statement.** "Extract the expiry date from the contract below and output an ISO 8601 date" beats "take a look at this contract" by twenty points or more. There is no substitute for this one.

**Two: a pinned output format.** Give an exact template or schema. Its contribution to accuracy is modest and its contribution to **consistency** is large — and downstream parse failures usually come from inconsistency, not inaccuracy.

**Three: few-shot examples.** Two to five. They convey what a wanted answer looks like, which prose usually describes at greater length and lower precision.

**Four: boundaries and refusal rules.** "If the contract states no expiry date, output null; do not guess." That one sentence often cuts hallucination more than any prompt trick, because it gives the model a legitimate exit for "I don't know".

**Five: chain-of-thought (task-dependent).** Only helps tasks that genuinely need multiple steps.

Meanwhile **role-play preambles and politeness** have almost no measurable benefit on modern models. They are not harmful, but when optimising a prompt they are the wrong thing to look at. The experiment above lets you see that ordering for yourself.

## Few-shot: why it works, when it hurts

The mechanism is not mysterious: examples directly demonstrate the input-to-output mapping, including the parts you cannot state precisely in language — tone, granularity, what to do at edge cases.

It has three known traps.

**Order bias.** Models are sensitive to example order, especially in classification: if the last example's label is "positive", the model leans slightly toward "positive". Mitigate by shuffling across several runs, or by balancing the label distribution among examples.

**Over-tight format binding.** If every example's input is a short sentence, the model flounders on a long paragraph. Examples should span the range of input shapes you actually see, not just the most typical one.

**Context crowding.** Five long examples can consume two thousand tokens, and multiplied by call volume that is real money. Which is precisely fine-tuning's third legitimate reason: **bake the stable examples into the weights and stop paying for them on every call.**

## Chain-of-thought: when it earns its keep

One reasonable account of why "let's think step by step" works: the model performs a fixed amount of computation per step, and writing out intermediate steps spreads the computation across more tokens — **it trades sequence length for computational depth.**

So the criterion is clear: **when a task needs multiple intermediate states, chain-of-thought helps; when one step suffices, it is a net loss.**

Extracting a date needs no deliberation; judging whether a contract satisfies seven risk rules does. On the former, chain-of-thought only adds cost, latency and fresh chances to go wrong — which is what the warning in the experiment reports.

One important modern footnote: many newer models have internalised the reasoning process during training (reasoning models), and for those you do not need — and sometimes should not — request step-by-step work in the prompt, since it can interfere with their own reasoning format. The right use of such a model is to state the problem clearly and let it think.

## Where the ceiling is

Prompt engineering always meets a ceiling, and locating it is what module four exists for. Three situations where a prompt cannot suffice:

1. **The behaviour you want cannot be stably described in language.** A tacit judgement convention, a domain expert's phrasing habits — you can give a hundred examples and cannot write five rules.
2. **There are too many rules to fit in context, or fitting them is too expensive.** Three thousand tokens of rules times a hundred thousand calls a day is a real annual budget line.
3. **You need a smaller, faster model.** No prompt makes a 1.5B model as capable as a 70B one, while fine-tuning sometimes can on a narrow task.

Those three map exactly onto the next module's three legitimate reasons to fine-tune. **Before you get there, get the prompt right and write its score down as your baseline.** Without one, you will never know where a post-fine-tuning gain came from.

## Exercises

1. In the experiment, start from nothing and add ingredients one at a time, recording accuracy and consistency after each. Identify the three with the best return — and note whether they match your intuition.
2. Take one of your own prompts, delete all role-play and politeness, count the tokens saved, then rerun to see whether quality moved. This exercise typically saves 10%–20% of input cost.
3. Write a "prompt baseline report" for a task of yours: the full prompt, the eval-set pass rate, tokens per call, and the specific reason you believe prompting cannot improve further. That report is the only sound basis for deciding whether to fine-tune.
