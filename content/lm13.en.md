## What fine-tuning actually changes

Start with the right mental model: **fine-tuning changes how the model behaves, not what it knows.**

Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) does exactly what pretraining does — minimise next-token cross-entropy. The only difference is the data: pretraining gets the whole internet, SFT gets a few thousand instances of "for this input, produce this output".

So fine-tuning fundamentally **nudges the model's output distribution toward your examples.** It is good at making the model imitate a manner; it is bad at making the model remember a pile of facts, because that would require repeating each fact dozens of times across a few thousand examples — an unreasonable sample efficiency.

## Three legitimate reasons

There are exactly three defensible reasons to fine-tune.

**One: the behaviour you want cannot be stably described in a prompt.**

The most common and most legitimate case. Example: making the model write conclusions the way your risk reviewers do — when to say "worth monitoring" rather than "presents risk", which details must be named, which conclusions must stay hedged. You can supply three hundred examples of such **tacit norms** and cannot write five rules for them. People learn the same way: a new reviewer shadows a senior one through six months of cases rather than memorising a manual.

**Two: you want to compress a large model's capability into a smaller one.**

Generate high-quality outputs with a large model and train a small one on them (distillation-style fine-tuning). On a narrow, well-defined task a fine-tuned 7B routinely catches a general large model at one or two orders of magnitude less cost per call. The economics are crisp: training is paid once, inference forever.

**Three: you want to retire a long prompt.**

If your prompt carries three thousand tokens of rules and examples, and those rules are stable (not revised monthly), baking them into the weights usually holds or improves quality while saving three thousand input tokens per call. At volume that is real money and real latency.

**Motives outside those three should almost always become retrieval or a better prompt.** Especially the commonest one — "make it know our products" — which is knowledge, and the previous chapter explained why.

## Catastrophic forgetting

This is fine-tuning's most insidious failure mode.

The mechanism is simple: you optimise repeatedly on a few hundred homogeneous examples, and any capability those examples never exercise (writing code, speaking other languages, refusing harmful requests) has no gradient signal supporting it, so it decays as the weights move.

**What makes it insidious is that your validation set cannot see it.** Your validation set comes from the same distribution as your training set, so it only reports that the target task improved. The fact that the model got worse at everything else surfaces when a user asks something slightly off-distribution.

Three mitigations:

1. **Use LoRA rather than full fine-tuning.** Moving a small subset of parameters damages existing capability far less.
2. **Mix general examples into the training data.** 5%–20% general instruction data is a common ratio.
3. **Keep a held-out evaluation set unrelated to your task** and run it after every fine-tune. It is the only way to see forgetting at all.

The third is a process requirement, not an option. In the next chapter's hyperparameter experiment we will watch that metric degrade with the learning rate first-hand.

## Baseline first

The last point, and the most frequently skipped: **before you fine-tune, the score of a prompt-only solution must be written down.**

The reason is practical. When the fine-tune finishes you need to answer one question: how much did it improve? Without a baseline that question has no answer, and teams usually substitute "it feels better now" — which, as module eight shows, is the most expensive sentence in this field.

A complete baseline includes: the full prompt, the eval-set pass rate, tokens and latency per call, and your specific reason for believing prompting has topped out. **A fine-tune without that document is not engineering; it is wishing.**

On sample size: empirically, **the minimum viable scale for behaviour fine-tuning is 500–1,000 highly consistent examples.** Below that, what you train will probably lose to a well-written prompt. If you have 100 today, the right move is to get to 500 — not to "train one and see".

## Exercises

1. Work through all six scenarios in the experiment, then write down three "we want to fine-tune" requests from your own project and classify each. Be honest — most people find at least one is really a knowledge problem.
2. Write a baseline report (the four items above) for the request you most want to fine-tune. Once written, your answer to "should we?" may well have changed.
3. Build a held-out set for detecting forgetting: 20 general questions entirely unrelated to your task (a few each of common sense, code, another language, and refusal boundaries). Build it now and every future fine-tune can use it.
