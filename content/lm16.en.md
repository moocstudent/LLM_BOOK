## The low-rank hypothesis

All of LoRA's mathematics fits on one line:

```
W' = W + ΔW,  where ΔW = (α/r) · B · A
```

W is the frozen original weight (d×d), A is r×d, B is d×r, and r is far smaller than d. Only A and B update during training.

**Why does this work?** Because of an empirical observation: **the weight change ΔW induced by fine-tuning is low-rank.**

The intuitive account is that what fine-tuning does carries very little information. You are teaching the model to answer in a format, or to judge by a convention — an adjustment along a direction, not relearning language. A 4096×4096 matrix has over sixteen million degrees of freedom, far more than expressing such an adjustment requires.

A rank-8 LoRA expresses it with two 4096×8 matrices: 65 thousand parameters, 0.4% of the original.

## Parameter count: strictly linear

The formula:

```
per adapted projection:  2 · r · d
whole model:             Σ(adapted projections) × layers
```

The key point is that it is **linear** in r and **first-order** in matrix size (while the original matrix is quadratic). That is where hundred-fold compression comes from.

The table in the experiment gives concrete numbers: a 7B model adapting only q and v at r=8 has about 4 million trainable parameters (0.06%) and an 8 MB adapter file. **Eight megabytes means you can commit it to git, email it to a customer, hot-load it in a server in milliseconds.** That number changed more than the quality did.

## α and r must be tuned together

α/r is the scaling factor applied to ΔW. Functionally it acts as a separate learning-rate multiplier for the LoRA branch.

Common practice is **α = r or α = 2r**, fixing the scale at 1 or 2. The benefit: when you change r, the magnitude of the update does not change with it — otherwise changing r silently changes the learning rate, and you cannot attribute a quality shift to either.

This is a real and common experimental error. Make it a rule: **change r, change α with it.**

One initialisation detail: A is randomly initialised and B is initialised to zero, so ΔW = 0 at the start and the model is exactly equivalent to the original. **Fine-tuning departs from a starting point that breaks nothing.** That design matters more than it looks.

## Which modules to adapt

This is the chapter's most practically valuable section, and the conclusion may be counter-intuitive: **module coverage matters more than rank.**

Specifically, `r=8 on all modules` usually beats `r=64 on q/v only` — and the latter has more trainable parameters.

The reason goes back to chapter one: attention moves information between positions while the FFN transforms it within a position, and **the model's knowledge and its "how to do it" live mostly in the FFN**. If your task only adjusts tone and format, q/v suffices; if you want a new domain reasoning pattern, leaving out the MLP often means it does not stick.

Practical guidance:

- **Adapt everything by default** (q, k, v, o, gate, up, down). The extra cost is small and it eliminates "did I pick the wrong modules?" as a debugging direction.
- **Start rank at 16.** Most tasks saturate between 8 and 32.
- **Use high rank (64+) in two cases only**: tens of thousands of examples, or teaching a genuinely new capability rather than a style.

Rank beyond 64 is wasteful on most tasks: quality saturated long ago while you pay extra memory and overfitting risk. Push r to 256 in the experiment and watch projected quality barely move while the parameter count grows 32×.

## LoRA's limits

So as not to leave you overly optimistic, three real limits:

1. **With very large datasets (a hundred thousand examples and up), full fine-tuning has the higher ceiling.** The low-rank assumption becomes a constraint when genuinely learning a new capability.
2. **Stacking two LoRAs usually does not work.** Each was trained against the base and there is no reason their update directions are orthogonal; summing them tends to interfere. To get both behaviours, retrain one adapter on mixed data.
3. **Too low a rank underfits.** r=1 or 2 genuinely is not enough on complex tasks.
