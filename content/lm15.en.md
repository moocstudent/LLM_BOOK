## Only three hyperparameters really matter

Fine-tuning's hyperparameter space is pleasantly small.

**One: learning rate.** The most consequential. LoRA's sweet spot runs roughly 1e-4 to 3e-4; full fine-tuning wants an order of magnitude less, 1e-5 to 3e-5. The difference is not mysticism: LoRA's update is scaled by α/r and only a small set of parameters is moving.

**Two: epochs.** On small datasets 2–3 is usually the ceiling. That number is smaller than most people assume.

**Three: effective batch size.** Equal to `batch × gradient accumulation × devices`. It mainly affects stability and throughput, and influences final quality less than the first two. Too small (<4) and gradient noise dominates; too large and one epoch on a small dataset is only a few steps, so the model learns nothing.

Everything else (warmup ratio, weight decay, optimizer choice, schedule shape) returns far less in fine-tuning than getting the first three right. **If your time is limited, spend all of it on the learning rate.**

## Two degradations that look different

The experiment in this chapter reports two metrics; keep them distinct.

**Overfitting**: training loss keeps falling while validation loss rises. The model is memorising the training set's wording. Symptom: perfect on the original items, broken by a rephrasing.

**Catastrophic forgetting**: the target-task score rises while the model gets worse at everything else. Symptom: our task is fine, but ask it anything else and it fabricates.

**The crucial difference: overfitting is visible to your validation set, forgetting is not.** Because the validation set shares the training distribution, it contains no "other tasks" at all.

Which is why every fine-tune must run two evaluations: the task validation set (for overfitting) plus a general held-out set (for forgetting). The 20 general questions the previous chapter asked you to build exist for this.

## Reading those two curves

The minimum of the dashed line (validation loss) in the experiment is your best checkpoint. Three typical shapes:

**Validation loss falling all the way to the end.** There is more to gain: add epochs or add data.

**Validation loss bottoming at epoch 2 then turning up.** The typical small-dataset case. **The right action is to keep the epoch-2 checkpoint, not the last one.** This matters a great deal and is often forgotten — many people use the final weights, which may be two epochs into overfitting.

**Validation loss rising from the start.** The learning rate is too high, or there is a serious data problem (input containing the answer, say).

## A usable starting configuration

If you are starting a LoRA fine-tune now, this configuration produces reasonable results in most cases:

```
learning rate     2e-4  (cosine decay to 10%)
warmup            5% of total steps
epochs            3     (but checkpoint every epoch)
effective batch   16    (batch 4 × accumulation 4)
LoRA r            16
LoRA α            32
target modules    q, k, v, o, gate, up, down  (all)
gradient clipping 1.0
precision         bf16
```

**Then tune exactly one thing: the learning rate.** Try 3× either side (6e-5 and 6e-4) and take whichever gives the lowest validation loss. Those three runs usually settle the configuration.

Note the choice to adapt all target modules — the next chapter shows that LoRA's module coverage matters more than its rank, and covering everything costs little.

## One easily overlooked practice

**Loss masking in the training data.** An SFT example contains a prompt and an answer, and you usually want loss computed on the answer only — computing it on the prompt too spends capacity learning to generate the user's question, which is not what you want.

Most training frameworks handle this by default, but not all. If you notice the model starting to write the user's next question after fine-tuning, check this setting.
